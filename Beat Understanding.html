Why do we still hear a "beat" above 10 Hz?

Many physics textbooks state that humans can only hear beats when the frequency difference ($\Delta f$) is less than 10 Hz to 15 Hz. This is an oversimplification! What they actually mean is that our brains can only resolve distinct, countable pulses (the slow "wah-wah-wah" sound) up to about 15 times per second. It is a limitation of human temporal resolution, not a limit of the physical waves.

So what happens when the difference gets larger than 10 Hz? The physical sound waves continue to interfere and modulate in amplitude exactly as they did before. However, our perception changes:

  • 15 Hz to 50 Hz (Roughness): Your ear can no longer count the individual pulses because they happen too fast. Instead, your brain interprets this rapid fluttering as a harsh, buzzing, or "rough" sound texture.
  • Above 50 Hz (Difference Tones / Tartini Tones): As the difference grows even larger and the volume is sufficiently loud, a fascinating biological phenomenon occurs. The non-linear mechanical properties of your inner ear (specifically inside the cochlea) literally distort the incoming sound. This distortion generates a brand new, continuous "phantom" pitch inside your ear equal to $f_1 - f_2$. You aren't hearing a rhythm anymore; you are hearing a completely new musical note!

In short: The mathematical wave interference never stops—only the way your brain interprets it changes!
